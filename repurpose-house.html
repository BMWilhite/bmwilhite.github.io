Repurpose House / Operations case study

Reducing Client Onboarding from 12–14 to 7–9 Business Days

Repurpose House · January 2021–May 2023

Working across teams, we reduced typical client onboarding timelines from approximately 12–14 business days to 7–9 business days, using better client preparation, faster handoffs, and existing team capacity rather than adding editing headcount.

01 / Case study

Context

Repurpose House transformed clients' existing long-form content into social media posts, videos, and other digital assets. Delivering the first set of assets required collaboration among sales, onboarding, content curation, editing, quality assurance (QA), client approvers, and, for some packages, a social media publishing team.

I joined as a Copywriter (January–March 2021), became Onboarding Manager (April 2021–September 2022), and then Implementation Manager (October 2022–May 2023). I continued copywriting and content curation alongside onboarding through October 2021. Across these roles, I investigated bottlenecks, maintained departmental SOPs, coordinated improvements across teams, and reported findings to executive leadership.

Our onboarding clock started when the client signed and ended at the first delivery milestone for their package. For clients purchasing publishing, that meant initial assets had been delivered, approved, and scheduled to go live. For packages without publishing, it meant the first assets had been provided to the client. The timeline was tracked in business days.

02 / Case study

Problem

Leadership asked me to investigate why new clients were waiting so long for their first deliverables. I examined where work stalled across content collection, onboarding, curation, editing, QA, and approvals.

  • Late client inputs. Clients sometimes arrived at discovery without their initial content or access to their social media accounts.
  • Slow meeting scheduling. Some clients scheduled their own two- to three-hour onboarding call a week or more after signing.
  • Production queue constraints. Content curation and editing handoffs could leave initial assets waiting, especially when the editing team was at capacity.
  • Approval friction. If the correct client decision-maker or communication channel had not been established, review and approval added further delay.

The combined effect was that a client could wait several weeks after signing before seeing their first content ready for use or publication. I saw an opportunity to improve the end-to-end process by eliminating several small, compounding sources of waiting time.

03 / Case study

Changes

1. Start preparation before the onboarding call

We encouraged sales to ask for the first piece of content earlier and, when possible, book the onboarding meeting before the sales conversation ended rather than relying on the client to schedule later.

We revised automated onboarding emails to make expectations clearer: provide initial content, complete the creative-preference questionnaire, and gather account credentials or access ahead of the call. During the onboarding call, I worked to collect outstanding materials and resolve account-access issues immediately whenever possible.

2. Make the first production handoffs faster

Once source content arrived, we coordinated with the assigned curator to prioritize initial client deliverables. The curator selected material for repurposing, wrote social post copy and headlines, then handed the work to editors for clipping and design templates. The workflow used prioritized requests to reduce waiting between curation and editing.

3. Use underutilized in-house editing expertise

A recurring bottleneck was the available editing capacity. We identified that members of the QA team had previously been editors and had bandwidth to help with overflow. Routing suitable editing work to those team members relieved the bottleneck without hiring additional editors.

4. Improve visibility, ownership, and approvals

We used the existing Premier Client Onboarding Tracker (PCOT), a stage-based project board, together with a spreadsheet tracking onboarding progress. PCOT already existed before I joined; Zapier-supported task assignments and clearer follow-up helped teams recognize and act on their next steps.

As the onboarding manager, I tracked each client's progress, followed up on stalled handoffs, and coordinated across teams. We also made approval expectations explicit before delivery, confirming who could approve content and how best to reach them.

04 / Case study

Results

Internal onboarding tracking showed that typical time from contract signing to the package-specific initial delivery milestone fell from approximately 12–14 business days to 7–9 business days.

The improvement came from earlier preparation, clearer coordination, faster handoffs, and better allocation of existing production capacity—not from adding editing headcount.

05 / Case study

Better discovery

In a separate review of approximately 20–30 recent client revision requests, I noticed a recurring concern: the content did not sound like the client. Generic questions about communication style were not giving creators enough useful direction.

I began asking clients what they would never say, using contrasting examples to draw out preferences, and requesting existing content when available. I translated those answers into clearer production instructions so creators had specific guidance about the client's voice.

This helped clarify expectations and production handoffs. Revision outcomes were not separately measured.

06 / Case study

Making the work repeatable

When I took responsibility for onboarding, the training and documentation did not adequately cover the work. As I learned the function and improved its workflows, I substantially revised departmental SOPs to include practical operating procedures, client discovery, and handoff guidance.

Before leaving, I spent approximately three weeks helping train my successor. I later answered occasional questions about unusual account-connection issues.

07 / Case study

Lessons

A process can appear efficient within individual departments while remaining slow across the entire customer journey. The biggest gains came from understanding where work waited between teams and addressing several small constraints together, rather than demanding that one team simply work faster.

I learned to look upstream for the source of problems, coordinate changes with the teams doing the work, and make the revised process clear enough for someone else to repeat. Better procedures matter when people can use them in day-to-day work and pass that knowledge on.

My contribution

I owned much of the client onboarding coordination, identified bottlenecks, tracked progress, strengthened follow-up, and helped implement improvements with colleagues in sales, curation, editing, QA, and publishing. The changes and results were a cross-functional team effort.
